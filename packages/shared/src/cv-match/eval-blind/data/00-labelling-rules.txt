Labelling rules for the blind evaluation set. Written before any data and before any run.
Date written: 2026-11-03. Author: one agent, blind to the matcher's vocabulary, rules, tests and earlier evaluation data.
All candidates, companies, schools and numbers are fictional. "Today" for every case is 2026-11-03.

The rules below (down to "File format") are copied from the fresh set's rules file. The notes after them are new.

Labels, per requirement line, as a careful recruiter reading the extracted text:

M = met. The CV shows it, in any wording: synonym, abbreviation, English, an equal or higher degree, or years
    worked out from the date ranges. A line naming a role or field needs work or study in that role or field.
U = unmet. The CV contradicts it, or an item a complete CV would normally state is absent (years, degree, field,
    a named licence or certificate, a named tool or skill, a language level).
K = unknown. A recruiter cannot judge it from this CV: soft traits with no concrete evidence; things CVs do not
    normally state (health, shifts, travel, own motorbike, criminal record) when the CV is silent; empty or unreadable text.
A = ambiguous. Careful recruiters would differ. Reported apart, never scored as right or wrong. Used for:
    - "Kinh nghiệm: Từ N năm" (no field named) when the years are there but in an unrelated job;
    - a bonus line ("ưu tiên") the CV does not meet: not holding a bonus is not failing a requirement;
    - a soft line backed only by the candidate's own claim;
    - a line with several parts where the CV shows only some, and it is a matter of judgement whether that is enough;
    - an equivalence recruiters argue over (for example B1 versus "đọc hiểu chuyên ngành").

Keyword stuffing: text pasted from the ad or a keyword dump with nothing in the work history behind it earns no credit.
A measurable item whose only evidence is the dump is U, bonus lines included. Soft lines stay K.

Messy extraction: a person reading the raw text can still work out broken layouts, lost accents and spaced-out letters,
so the labels follow the content. Empty or undecodable text is K on every line and tier "maybe": a recruiter would open
the original file rather than reject it.

Tiers are a recruiter's view of the whole CV against the whole ad, not a count of labels:
interview = should interview, maybe = could go either way, no = should not interview.

File format. "@req <category> [bonus] | <line>" lines give the ad. Each CV starts with
"=== id | kind | difficulty | tier | labels", optionally "| nfd" (text served in decomposed Unicode, as some PDF tools
produce). Labels follow the order of the @req lines. Lines beginning "#why " are the labeller's notes and are not CV text.

Notes added for this set (2026-11-03)

Synthetic, single author. One author wrote everything: the ads, the CVs and every label. It was one agent, which wrote
the first ad itself and used parallel copies of itself, under these same rules, for the others. That is not a second
opinion: no independent labeller has checked any of it, and nobody measured agreement between labellers. This set can
compare one matcher version with another. It is not a substitute for real CVs read by real recruiters, and a good result
on it says little about real candidates until that has been checked.

How much was re-checked. The parallel copies were stopped by a usage limit around the end of their work, and none of them
reported finishing the planned read-back of labels against CVs. Afterwards the author ran a structure check on all files and
read 24 of the 120 CVs against their labels, mostly the partial, near-miss and messy ones. That found 4 labels of about 420
that contradicted the CV (1 @req label, 3 duty labels), all corrected. The other 96 CVs got the structure and pattern checks
only, so expect a similar small rate of label slips there, and more often on duties than on @req lines.

Blindness. While writing, the author read only the fresh set's rules file and the first 30 lines of its first data file
(for the format). The author read no matcher code, no tests, no other evaluation data and no run output, and never ran
the matcher. Each label was decided from the ad line and the whole CV, as a recruiter would, with no view on how a
machine would read it.

Real names. People, companies, schools and buildings are invented; any match with a real one is an accident. Real product
and standard names (AutoCAD, Revit, SAP, ECUS, VNACCS, MISA, TOEIC, IELTS, Incoterms, equipment brands) stay because real
ads and CVs use them. They are not part of anyone's personal data. Phones look like 0900 000 xxx, emails use @example.com.

Kinds:
    strong    = fits the ad; often worded very differently from it.
    partial   = fits part of the ad, with real gaps.
    near-miss = neighbouring job or field that looks right at a glance and falls short on the core of the ad.
    non-fit   = different job. Some were chosen because their content shares words with the ad by accident
                (for example M&E as Monitoring & Evaluation, or a data warehouse against a goods warehouse).
    stuffing  = pasted ad text or a keyword dump with no work history behind it.
    messy     = broken PDF extraction (interleaved columns, spaced-out letters, lost accents, broken lines). Any tier.
Kind says how the CV looks. Tier says what a recruiter would do with it. They are separate judgements.

CV text. CVs are written to be 1,500-4,000 characters so that unrelated content is present, as it is in a real CV. Some are
in Vietnamese without diacritics or in English by the candidate's choice. That is style, not a kind. No CV here is empty or
undecodable, and none uses the "nfd" flag, so those two rules from the copied section have no cases in this set.

Reading the requirement rules on long CVs. Evidence counts wherever it sits in the CV, but a skills-section list on its own
with a work history that contradicts it is weighed as a recruiter would weigh it: a named tool in an ordinary skills list is
accepted when the work history makes it plausible. A long list pasted from the ad is stuffing.

Job description labels (eval-blind/duties/<ad>.json, shape { "duties": [...], "labels": { "<cvId>": [...] } }). Labels follow
the order of "duties". There are three values and no A, and silence means something different from the @req lines:
    M = the CV's work history shows this kind of work, in any wording.
    U = the CV clearly shows the candidate did not do it or could not (for example "do kế toán trưởng phụ trách").
    K = the CV is silent, or a recruiter cannot tell.
A duty absent from a CV is K, not U. A duty that appears only in a keyword dump or a pasted ad, with nothing in the work
history, is K. Work in a clearly different field that only shares the words is K unless the CV itself rules it out. The
work counts at the level the CV shows it: real help on a duty is M, but "chỉ hỗ trợ, không trực tiếp làm" is U.
